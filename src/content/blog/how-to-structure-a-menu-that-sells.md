A menu is not a list of what you sell. It is the order in which you offer it, and that order changes what people choose.

## Sections do most of the work

People do not read a menu top to bottom. They scan for the section that matches what they came for, then read inside it. That means your section names and their order matter more than the order of items within them.

Two practical consequences:

**Name sections by what people want, not by kitchen logic.** "Breakfast" beats "Morning offerings". "Sharing plates" beats "Mezze selection" if your customers are not familiar with the term. Clarity converts.

**Put your strongest section first.** If your reputation is built on grills, do not open with soup because that is the traditional order. On a phone especially, the first screen is disproportionately valuable.

## Keep sections to about five to seven items

Long sections cause a specific problem: people stop comparing and start defaulting. Faced with fourteen mains, a lot of diners will pick something familiar rather than read all of them.

Shorter sections force you to make the choice you should be making anyway — which of these actually sells, and which is on the menu because it always has been.

If you genuinely have more, split the section. "Mains" becomes "From the grill" and "From the sea". Two sections of six read faster than one of twelve.

## Descriptions: one line, and make it earn its place

The rule that works: describe the thing the name does not already tell you.

- "Caesar salad" — everyone knows. Say what is different: "with grilled chicken and anchovy dressing".
- "Chef's special pasta" — nobody knows. This needs a full description.
- "Cheeseburger" — the description should be doing the selling: what cheese, what bun, what is on it.

Long descriptions on every item make a menu exhausting. Descriptions on the items that need them make the menu useful.

## Prices

Two things are worth getting right.

**Drop the currency symbol and the trailing zeros.** "48" reads more easily than "AED 48.00". The section header or the menu already establishes the currency. This is not a trick, it is just less visual noise.

**Do not right-align prices in a column with dotted leaders.** A neat column of prices invites people to read the column instead of the food — comparing on price rather than on what they want. Put the price immediately after the item, at the end of the line, where it is found rather than scanned.

On a digital menu this is largely handled for you, but it is worth knowing why the layout is the way it is.

## What changes on a phone

Some printed-menu advice does not transfer.

**The "golden triangle"** — the idea that eyes go to the upper right of a printed page first — does not apply. Phone menus are read vertically, top to bottom. Position value is simply: earlier is better.

**Boxing an item** to draw attention works on paper and looks like an advertisement on a phone. Use it once, if at all.

**Menu length stops being a physical constraint** and becomes an attention constraint. You can now list a hundred items. You still should not.

**Photos become available**, and they change the ranking logic entirely. An item with a photo will outsell the item above it without one. That is a tool — use it on the dishes you want to sell, not indiscriminately on everything.

## The thing digital menus let you do that paper never could

You can find out if any of this is true for your venue.

Change the order of a section, leave it a fortnight, and look at what people viewed and what the kitchen actually sent out. Move a dish up. Add a photo to the one you want to shift. Take off the three items nobody has ordered since spring.

On a printed menu, every one of those experiments costs a reprint, so nobody runs them. That is why most menus are laid out the way they are — not because it was optimal, but because changing it was expensive.

It is not expensive any more. The menu can be a thing you tune rather than a thing you print.
